The hour I read before I write

People ask where the writing comes from. The honest answer: it comes from the hour before the writing.

Every day I read for thirty minutes before I open the notebook. Not research, not "content" — books. Noir, mostly. A chapter of Chandler when I want to remember how a sentence can do two jobs at once. A little Haruf when I want to watch a writer trust quiet moments. Sometimes nature writing — Berry, Dillard — when I need to remember that a book can be about paying attention.

It's a warm-up. You don't sprint before you jog; you don't write before you read.

The notebook

I keep a Moleskine. Not because it's fashionable — because it's the right size for a coat pocket and the pages stay open on a kitchen counter.

I edit standing up. The kitchen counter is the right height for it, and standing keeps me honest: if a sentence can't survive me pacing while I read it aloud, it gets cut.

The origin story

I started at a deli counter. Slicing meat, wrapping packages, listening to regulars talk about their week. Then the library. Then night shifts as a proofreader, which is where I learned that most of writing is deleting.

My grandmother gave me a word search when I was seven. She had torn it out of a magazine, folded it into her purse, and handed it to me at a bus stop. I finished it before the bus came. She was the one who told me, years later, that making a puzzle for someone is a way of saying I thought about you while I was alone.

I still believe that. It's why every book ends with a thank-you note.

Why word searches first

I'm a writer across genres — fiction is the destination. But a writer needs readers, and readers need to trust you. Word searches are the ship-now genre: they're useful, they're calm, they're a small thing done well.

So I write large-print puzzle books for adults and seniors, and I keep the fiction in the notebook. Every book is practice. Every book is a promise to write a better one.

The thank-you

If you've made it this far: thank you for reading. It means more than you know — I was thinking about you while I was alone.
